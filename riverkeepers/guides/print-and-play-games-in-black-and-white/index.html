Print and play games in black and white

You can print a print-and-play game in black and white when every game state remains distinguishable without color. Check the rules and a grayscale print preview before using paper: if two pieces or spaces are identified only by color, look for an official monochrome file or another game.

Check meaning before checking ink

A color setting in the print dialog does not tell you whether a game will remain playable. Find what its colors mean in the rules, then inspect the actual grayscale pages. A label or symbol that survives printing is more reliable than two shades that look alike.

Do not assume a color game will work after grayscale conversion. Its own rules and pages decide that, especially when cards, player factions, or terrain types use color as information.

A short black-and-white print test

  1. Read the component list. Check which pages are needed to play and whether a separate monochrome or low-ink file is supplied.
  2. Open the actual play sheet. Locate every key symbol, track, and place where you will write. Compare those marks with the rules.
  3. Choose black-and-white or grayscale in the print dialog. Check the page size and scale recommended by that game's maker. Preview every needed page before committing the print job.
  4. Test one page. Check that the smallest labels, shaded areas, and write-in spaces remain distinct on paper. If they do not, change the print setting or use the game's official alternative.

Grayscale does not automatically mean low ink: a full-page gray background may still print heavily. A purpose-made line-art sheet is easier to check than a color page converted at the last moment.

A black-and-white map example

Riverkeepers' two play sheets are designed in black and white for A4 or US Letter paper. The Past sheet labels river column D, riverside columns C and E, and rows 1–6. It uses S for Settlement, L for Levee, and B for Bridge; a ruined Bridge is marked B×. Flooded bank cells are shaded, so the mark and its position matter more than a color name.

On the Future sheet, a dry Settlement becomes T for Town and a flooded one becomes R for Ruins. A broken Bridge becomes XB; roads and Marsh have their own marks. These printed labels help you read the map even with a monochrome printer, but you still need to check your own print for clear shading and room to write.

For this game, print both sheet pages one-sided at 100% scale with browser headers and footers off. If your printer clips an edge, use “fit to printable area” and recheck the preview. FAQ

Can every print-and-play game be printed in black and white?

No. A game that uses color alone to distinguish pieces or spaces may become hard to play in grayscale. Check its rulebook and actual pages, and look for an official monochrome file when color carries game information.

Does grayscale always save ink?

No. Converting a dark background to gray can still put a lot of ink on the page. Inspect the preview and prefer an official low-ink or line-art version when one is available.

What if pale lines disappear on my printer?

Print a single test page and compare it with the screen version. Try the printer's available quality or contrast settings, or use the game's official alternate sheet. Do not start a session until the grid and its labels are readable.

Are Riverkeepers' sheets color dependent?

No. They are black-and-white sheets with written symbols and labeled map columns. You still need both pages, a pencil, and two six-sided dice for the complete game.
