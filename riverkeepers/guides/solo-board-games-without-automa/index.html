How to tell if a solo game has an automa

Open the game's official solo rules and follow one full round. If the rules make you take actions for a simulated rival—often using an automa deck, bot board, or decision chart—the solo mode has an automa or equivalent bot. A weather card or random event that only changes your own puzzle is not, by itself, an opponent. A “1 player” label cannot settle the question.

A three-step check in the rulebook

  1. Find the official solo setup. Check the publisher's rulebook or game download for a one-player section. Search terms such as “automa,” “bot,” “AI,” and “solo opponent,” then see whether a separate deck, board, or chart is required. A component list alone may not say what that component does.
  2. Trace one complete round. After your action, does the rulebook tell you to reveal a rival card, move another side's pieces, or resolve an opponent turn? If so, you will manage a simulated opponent even if the rules use another name for it. If you only resolve a neutral event or timer, keep reading before calling it a bot.
  3. Check the goal and ending. Note whether you compare against a rival, a score threshold, an objective, or a fixed ending. A score target can coexist with an automa, so use the turn sequence—not scoring alone—to make the final call.

If the solo rules are unavailable, the product's player count is insufficient evidence. Look for a publisher playthrough or ask for the solo setup and turn order before deciding.

Apply the check to Riverkeepers

Riverkeepers is written for one player. Its full game has a Past sheet and a Future sheet, a pencil, and two six-sided dice. There is no opponent deck or simulated player: dice determine rows, Water, road Steps, and occasional events; you choose where to build and draw roads.

On a blank Past map, suppose you roll 2 and 5. You can choose 2 as the Row and place a Settlement at C2, which is empty riverside land; the other die adds 5 to Water. Or choose row 5 for a legal build and add 2 Water. A flood mark is reached only when the Water Track gets to 9, 18, 27, or 36, so this first turn causes no flood in either choice.

After eight Past turns, you copy the map to the Future sheet. If at least one Town survived, you play eight Future road turns and score your strongest connected Town network, Relics, and Hardship, then record an ending. If no Town survived, the rules end the game with The Drowned Valley and score 0. See the full game and what the download includes.

FAQ

Does “1 player” mean there is no automa?

No. A game can support one player by giving that player an opponent deck to run. Read the solo setup and turn order to find out what you actually have to manage.

Is a random event table the same as an automa?

Usually not. An event table changes the state of the game; an automa takes actions for a simulated rival. Riverkeepers' doubles can trigger an event, but there is no separate opponent turn.

Must a solo game without an automa have a win-or-lose target?

No. Some games finish with a score, outcome, or ending rather than a pass/fail target. Riverkeepers has five named endings and a final score, with no separate win-or-lose rule.

Can I just play a multiplayer game by myself?

Only if its rules support that approach or you accept making your own variant. A game built around hidden information, trading, or an opponent's decisions may lose its core challenge when one person controls every side. Check for an official solo mode first.
